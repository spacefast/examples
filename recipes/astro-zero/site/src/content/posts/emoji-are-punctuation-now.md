There's a certain kind of person who thinks the reaction button cheapened the
internet. Before reactions, they'll tell you, if you had something to say you
had to *say* it. Now you can tap a flame and feel like you participated.

I used to be that person. Then I looked at the numbers on a site I ran and found
that reactions weren't replacing comments. They were replacing *nothing at all*.
The people tapping had never been going to write a paragraph. They were the
silent ninety percent, and for the first time I could see them.

## The cost curve of saying something

Every way of responding to a piece of writing has a price, and the prices are
wildly far apart.

Writing a considered comment costs maybe three minutes and a small amount of
social exposure: your sentence is now sitting under someone else's work with
your name on it, permanently, where it can be misread.

Tapping an emoji costs about four hundred milliseconds and nothing else.

That gap is enormous, and for most of the web's history there was nothing in it.
You either paid the full price or you left. Reactions filled the gap, and the
thing that filled it turned out to be where nearly everyone lives.

## They're not a downgrade, they're a different unit

The instinct to rank reactions below comments assumes they're measuring the same
thing badly. They're not measuring the same thing at all.

A comment is an argument. It has a claim, it invites a reply, it can be wrong.
Its value is in its content.

A reaction is closer to a laugh in a room. It carries almost no content and a
lot of information: how many people are here, whether the thing landed, whether
the room found it funny or found it grim. You cannot get that from four
comments, however good they are. Four comments tell you what four people think.
Two hundred taps tell you the shape of the room.

Punctuation works the same way. A period carries no semantic content and you'd
never write without one.

## Designing them so they mean something

Reactions go wrong in predictable ways, and all of them come from treating the
palette as free.

**Too many options.** Twelve emoji is not more expressive, it's a decision. The
whole value proposition was that this costs nothing to do; make someone browse a
grid and you've priced it back up to the cost of a short comment without any of
the payoff. Four is generous. Six is pushing it.

**No way to change your mind.** If the tap is permanent, people hesitate, and
hesitation is exactly the friction you were trying to remove. Let them take it
back. Let them move it. A reaction should be as cheap to undo as it was to make.

**Counts that only go up.** An unmoderated, unremovable, monotonically
increasing number is a slot machine, not a signal. It rewards the thing that
already won.

**A palette without a point of view.** The four you choose are an editorial
statement about what kinds of response this site expects. Applause, surprise,
sympathy, and heat say something quite different from thumbs up, thumbs down,
heart, and star. Pick on purpose.

## The honest version

Here's what I'd tell my earlier, snobbier self: your comment section was never a
measure of whether people cared. It was a measure of how many people were
willing to pay three minutes and some exposure to prove it. That's a real
signal, but it's a narrow one, and treating it as the whole picture meant
treating the overwhelming majority of your readers as though they weren't there.

They were there. They just didn't have a way to nod.
